Swingbys, part 1: how they work, and which bodies pay

Changing a spacecraft's velocity by kilometres per second without spending a drop of fuel. We follow, in numbers, what is going on and which bodies used which way pay off most.

The velocity changes with no burn at all

Pass close to a body and gravity bends your direction of travel. What matters here is whose point of view you take.

Seen from the body, the spacecraft comes in and leaves along a hyperbola. Arriving and departing, the speed is exactly the same. Only the direction changes. Whatever speed gravity added on the way in is given back on the way out. The grey dashed line is “where it would have gone without the bend”, and the departure from it is the turn angle.

Seen from the Sun it is a different story. The body itself is running around the Sun, so the spacecraft's velocity is “the body's velocity plus the velocity seen from the body”. Turn one of those without changing its length and the sum still changes. Here 30.06 became 33.43 km/s. That is a swingby.

Pass on the other side and the same thing works in reverse.

The incoming speed and the turn angle are exactly as before. The only difference is which way it bends. That alone drops 30.06 to 26.25 km/s. The 3.37 gained and the 3.81 lost both come from exchanging energy with the body.

The energy comes from the body Conservation of energy is not being broken. What you gain is taken from the body's orbital motion. But Earth is some 10 20 times heavier than the spacecraft, so the amount by which Earth's orbit slows is far too small to measure.

It can speed you up or slow you down

In the right-hand figure above, the tip of the outgoing V∞moves along a circle. Come round on the side the body is heading and you speed up; the other side and you slow down. Which side you pass decides it.

In the app, a swingby sequence has a field called rotation angle β . That is “which side of the body you pass”. Build an orbit that returns to Earth in a year (V∞ 4.02 km/s), hold the periapsis altitude at 16000 km, and vary β alone.

The speed does not change at β = 0 because the way V∞ turns is exactly perpendicular to the orbital direction. Instead, the orbit plane tilts. This orbit is in fact lifted 0.13 AU (about 7 degrees) out of the ecliptic. When you want to change the plane, this is what you use.

Slowing down is a proper use in its own right. Missions heading for Mercury or the Sun slow at Venus or Earth to fall inward (that is tutorial ②).

How far it can bend

Only three things set the turn angle: the body's gravity μ, the closest distance r p, and the incoming speed V∞.

The turn-angle formula sin(δ/2) = 1 / (1 + r p V∞² / μ)
The stronger the gravity, the closer you pass, and the slower you go, the more it bends.

That slower bends more is the part that snags. Go past faster and gravity has less time to pull on you. That is all it is.

Turn angle above, the change in velocity as seen from the Sun, |Δv| , below. Both are taken at the closest altitude that body allows.

The thing to notice is that the lower graph has a peak. Approach slowly and it bends well, but there is little speed to bend. Approach fast and there is plenty to bend, but it hardly bends. The balance sets the most profitable speed.

Where the peak sits |Δv| = 2 V∞ sin(δ/2) is largest at V∞ = √(μ/r p) . That is the speed of a circular orbit at that altitude, exactly. And the turn angle there is exactly 60 degrees, with the |Δv| you get also being √(μ/r p).

The energy you receive

More than how many km/s the velocity changed, how much the orbital energy changed is what bears directly on how far you can go. The change in energy seen from the Sun can be written like this.

The change in energy ΔE = (the body's orbital speed) × (the component of Δv along that orbital direction)
In other words, the faster a body orbits, the more you receive for the same Δv. The units are km²/s² — the same as launch energy C3.

The peak in turn angle, multiplied by orbital speed, gives the next figure. Across is the speed of approach to that body; up is the most energy a single swingby can give you.

Venus and Earth level off around V∞ 7–8 km/s , at 230 to 250. Jupiter climbs and climbs, reaching 449 at V∞ 34 km/s. That is more than the 443.6 it takes to break a 1 AU circular orbit free of the Sun. A Jupiter swingby can throw a spacecraft out of the solar system because its gravity is in another class.

The faster the orbit, and the heavier and smaller the body (so you can pass closer), the better. Venus and Earth rank high because they orbit fast; Jupiter is first because its gravity is in another league. As a result, Earth and Venus pay in the inner solar system, and Jupiter in the outer. Mars and Pluto rank low because their gravity is too small to bend anything.

Is faster always better?

Look at the three right-hand columns. The ranking changes with the speed of approach.

  • V∞ 5 km/s , Venus (235) and Earth (210) beat Jupiter (128) handily.
  • V∞ 10 km/s , Jupiter (241) overtakes Venus (237). The crossover is around V∞ 9.9 km/s.
  • V∞ 15 km/s , Jupiter (329) runs away with it while Venus (195) is already on the way down.

In other words, “Approach a good body fast” is not enough on its own. The inner planets level off at 7–8 km/s, so going faster than that loses you ground. Jupiter and Saturn, on the other hand, are still climbing across the speeds you can actually reach, so the faster you arrive the better.

Furthest from the curve is New Horizons, in ③. It passes 2.33 million km from Jupiter's centre. The closest allowed is 107 thousand km, so it is 22 times further out than it need be. At closest approach at the same speed it could have taken 375; it takes 66.

This is of course not a failure. Jupiter has a fierce radiation belt around it, and going close breaks the spacecraft. Besides, it has to be bent in the right direction for Pluto, and how much you take is settled by where you are aiming. A swingby is not about taking as much energy as possible; it is about taking what you need, in the direction you need it.

In short

  • Seen from the body, the speed does not change — only the direction. Seen from the Sun, the body's velocity is added, so the speed does.
  • Which side you pass (the rotation angle β) makes it a gain or a loss. Under identical conditions the spread is 5.1 km/s.
  • Slower bends better, but there is less to bend. The best is at V∞ = √(μ/r p), where the turn angle is exactly 60 degrees.
  • The energy you receive is orbital speed × Δv. Venus and Earth level off at 7–8 km/s; Jupiter climbs to 34 km/s and reaches 449.
  • So: a slow spacecraft wants the inner planets, a fast one wants Jupiter. The crossover is around V∞ 9.9 km/s.

What part 2 covers: raising V∞ before you use it

So far we have seen that Venus and Earth make excellent swingby partners: they orbit fast (35.0 and 29.8 km/s) and let you pass just above the atmosphere. And that they give their best at V∞ 7–8 km/s.

There is one more ceiling that tells. However well you do it, the speed leaving a swingby can never exceed “the body's orbital speed + V∞”. That is the value with all of V∞ pointed along the orbital direction, and nothing can add more.

Speed
Speed needed to head for Jupiter from 1 AU38.58 km/s
Earth's orbital speed29.78 km/s
→ V∞ needed for an Earth swingby to throw you out to Jupiter 8.79 km/s or more

But leave Earth straightforwardly and come back to Earth about a year later, and V∞ only reaches around 5 km/s. The ceiling is 29.78 + 5.3 = 35.08 km/s. However well it is bent, that does not reach Jupiter.

So you must raise V∞ before you get back to Earth. There are two ways.

  • Raise it with a burn on the way. Juno, in tutorial ④, did this: a 670 m/s burn lifted the V∞ on return from 5.3 to 9.9 km/s. The ceiling becomes 39.68 km/s, which reaches Jupiter. That is ΔVEGA .
  • Raise it by using another body first. Venus gives 235 even at V∞ 5 km/s, making it a more efficient “V∞ amplifier” than Earth (210) under the same conditions. Putting one in is VEGA, and using Earth twice gives VEEGA .
